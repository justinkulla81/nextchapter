### What the two sets show
Trades money is specific: named states, partners, credentials and job guarantees, in programs of $50 million to $115 million. White-collar money is mostly broad pledges (billions, targets in the millions) spread across many purposes; the one program for laid-off professionals we found is Verizon's $20 million for its own former employees. Outcome evidence is thin for both and mostly self-reported. The independent benchmark (Anthropic's review of 56 randomized studies) does not split the groups. **Our reading, not a finding:** announced dollars are concentrated where the AI buildout needs trades, while the Census data below show white-collar workers are a larger share of employment and of the long-term unemployed.

## 2. Local data centers

Data centers are where AI spending meets a town's labor market, and the local picture is about construction and trades more than office work.

- **Jobs.** Kansas City: Meta's Northland site (about $1 billion) about 1,500 construction jobs at peak and about 100 permanent; Nebius in Independence about 1,200 and about 130 **[reported, KCUR, Mar 22, 2026]**. A Brookings county study finds roughly 100 to 200 jobs in a typical affected county, wages unchanged and home prices up 2% to 5% **[reported, May 2026]**. No source gives a national white-collar versus blue-collar split.
- **Local fights.** County pauses: Hill County, TX (May 12, 2026, one year), Otoe County, NE (May 2026), Montgomery County, MD (18 months, July 2026, one advocacy source **[weak]**). Reasons given: water, electricity prices, tax breaks. Economist/YouGov (Aug 28 to 31, 2026): 63% oppose a new data center in their community, 19% support **[reported]**. Virginia's sales-tax exemption costs about $1.9 billion a year and, per a newspaper summary of the state review, yields about 84 jobs per $1 million of exemption **[reported; study not read]**.
- **Retraining tied to data centers** is in the blue-collar table above (Meta, Google.org, Louisiana Delta, Gateway Technical College in Racine, WI: 206 enrolled and 14 certificates earned by end of May **[reported]**).
- **What this shows.** Retraining reaches technicians and tradespeople; permanent jobs are few; local opposition centers on water, power costs and tax breaks. It does not show any effect on white-collar employment: engineers, architects and project managers work ahead of construction and we have no count. We will track open postings for those roles.

## 4. The public debate and the data

Camps below are the owner's starting groupings, with the voice's own position where it differs. Every position is as reported; "label" is how well we could read the source.

**Acceleration / optimist-leaning.**
- **All-In podcast** (Chamath Palihapitiya, David Sacks, Jason Calacanis, David Friedberg): the May 29, 2026 episode's segments frame the job-loss story as a "narrative" that has "flipped" and include one on "making yourself valuable in the age of AI" **[official, show notes only; no host quotes verified]**. We found no verified 2026 individual jobs quote from Sacks; his Aug 2025 post, as reported, called job-loss predictions "overhyped as AGI itself" **[reported, 2025]**.
- **Jensen Huang:** job-loss fears are exaggerated; AI automates tasks and creates jobs; no new policy **[reported]**.
- **Marc Andreessen:** "technology doesn't destroy jobs and never will" **[official, as quoted by Carnegie, Apr 2026]**; layoffs reflect overstaffing **[reported]**.
- **Tyler Cowen** (May 22, 2026): AI "will not bring mass unemployment" but will change most jobs; it will "feel bad for a while" for those who lose; individuals should build human and interpersonal skills; no government program stated **[official]**.
- **Reid Hoffman** accepts a painful transition ("the intermediate transition is very painful," 2025) **[reported]**.

**Warning / risk-focused.**
- **Ezra Klein** (NYT column, May 2026): not an apocalypse, but moderate losses may be "the possibility we're least prepared for"; past retraining was often "some retraining options that don't work"; points to COVID-era income support as a precedent **[official text]**.
- **Derek Thompson** (Plain English): Feb 2026 "America Isn't Ready for What AI Will Do to Jobs"; May 2026 episode with economist Alex Imas on "the case against the AI job apocalypse," so he now hosts both views **[official]**.
- **Sam Harris** (Making Sense #479 with Vinod Khosla, Jun 4, 2026): topics include "the prospect of mass job displacement" and a "policy framework to redistribute AI's gains"; positions not verified **[official, topic summary only]**.
- **Dario Amodei, Geoffrey Hinton** ("massive unemployment," Jan 2026, **[reported]**; rejects universal basic income as insufficient), **Tristan Harris** ("be way more worried about AI" than immigration, **[reported, Fortune Feb 2026]**), **Bernie Sanders' office** (32-hour week, robot tax; the "97 million jobs" figure came from asking ChatGPT, **[weak]**).

**Skeptics and middle.**
- **Gary Marcus:** AI "may be serving as a fig leaf" for layoffs driven by financial underperformance **[reported]**. **Daron Acemoglu:** steer AI toward pro-worker uses through tax, antitrust and education **[weak]**. **Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor:** AI-cited layoffs are often "a convenient excuse"; change will be slow **[weak/reported]**. **Paul Krugman:** technology has not produced mass unemployment **[weak]**. **Kathryn Anne Edwards** (Platformer, Jun 2, 2026): "some job loss, some job change, and some new jobs"; rebuild unemployment insurance, retraining in shortage fields, relocation help, no universal basic income **[official]**. **Molly Kinder** (Humane Tech podcast, Aug 13, 2026): "as long as that debate stays alive, nobody plans or takes action"; plan for a "messy middle" **[official]**.

**What the public thinks.** Pew (June 2026): 71% expect fewer jobs over 20 years, 64% in 2024; Quinnipiac (March 2026): 70%, 30% of employed adults worry about their own job, and white-collar (71%) and blue-collar (73%) workers are about equally pessimistic; Bentley-Gallup (May 2026): 79% expect fewer jobs in 10 years; Gallup workers (Feb 2026): 18% say their own job is likely eliminated within five years, up from 15% **[official/reported]**.

**What the data show.** Stanford (Aug 12, 2026): employment for ages 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations is about 19% below trend, through fewer hires, and "we do not see widespread, economy-wide job displacement"; descriptive, not causal **[official]**. Yale's Budget Lab and Brookings: "stability, rather than disruption" **[official, Oct 2025]**; New York Fed: "changing skill requirements" more than eliminating jobs **[official]**; Challenger: AI cited for 121,401 of 617,189 announced cuts in 2026 but 1,265 in October, which is what employers say **[reported]**.

**How the camps fare.** Worry about jobs generally is high and about one's own job much lower; the "public is anxious" claim holds. "Fear is overblown" cannot be tested, since polls measure worry, not whether it is warranted. "No economy-wide disruption so far" is supported by Stanford, the New York Fed and Yale. "Entry-level jobs are at risk" is partly supported (Stanford's hiring gap), not as proof of cause. **Our reading, not a finding:** the debate is mostly about a future the data cannot yet test.

**Writers and authors (not only podcasters).**
- **Nick Bostrom** rejects the jobs-anxiety frame: his book *Deep Utopia* (2024) asks what gives life meaning in a "solved world" where AI does nearly everything better. In a 2024 interview he said shorter work weeks did not happen because "we just have chosen to buy more stuff." We found no 2025 or 2026 essay of his on AI and jobs and could not verify a reported October 2026 newspaper piece, so we do not cite one **[reported, The Independent via AOL, Apr 20, 2024]**.
- **Daniel Susskind** (essay, Knight First Amendment Institute, Apr 7, 2025): "there is no economic law that says this outcome must be so," meaning we should not assume human work or high wages will necessarily remain; he takes public concern seriously and proposes no specific policy there **[official]**. A 2026 book is mentioned in a search snippet only **[weak]**.
- **Gary Marcus** (Substack, Apr 1, 2026, "On employment, don't panic, yet"): productivity and return on AI investment "has so far been modest"; employers should use AI to support staff, not replace them **[official]**.
- **Ethan Mollick** (One Useful Thing, Jun 30, 2026): "work is increasingly about assigning work to agents"; advice is to delegate multistep work, supervise it and build on existing expertise; the post does not address worker anxiety **[official]**.
- **Derek Thompson** (newsletter, Feb 13, 2026): "it's hard to see any effect of AI at all" in the current labor market, while arguing America is not prepared **[official, paywalled]**.
- **Noah Smith** (Noahpinion, Jul 20, 2025): "AI has not yet had a detectably negative effect on the job market"; notes that the average American is negative about the technology **[official, paywalled, 2025]**.
- **Cory Doctorow** (Pluralistic, Jan 5, 2026) argues AI is sold to executives as a way to remove workers who resist profit-maximizing **[weak, summary only]**. **Matt Shumer** (Feb 9, 2026, "Something Big Is Happening") wrote that AI does his own technical work and "your job could be next," which Fortune and Gary Marcus criticized as overstated **[weak]**.
- **Erik Brynjolfsson** (Stanford, Aug 12, 2026) is a research paper, not an essay; see the data paragraph above.
- Carnegie Endowment's April 2026 roundup sorts writers into three views on the future of work (the excited, the alarmed, the patient) and is a useful map **[official roundup]**.
- Could not verify a 2026 source for Harari, Martin Ford, Kevin Kelly, Kai-Fu Lee, Kurzweil, Ted Chiang, Jaron Lanier, Henry Farrell, Cal Newport, Yudkowsky and Soares, Karen Hao, Brian Merchant, Annie Lowrey, Scott Alexander or Zvi Mowshowitz; they are not described here.

What writers add beyond podcasts: dated, checkable claims (Brynjolfsson's 19%, Klein's poll); arguments about measurement and framing (Marcus on layoff attribution; "descriptive, not causal"); concrete task-level advice for workers (supervise agents, build on expertise, human-connection work) with few public-policy programs; and a long-run question about meaning (Bostrom, Susskind) outside the near-term jobs data.

## 5. The canary data we track

Four monthly Census CPS series back to 2010 (new graduates 22 to 27, white-collar under 35, white-collar 50 and over, all workers), plus job cuts citing AI by country and a ten-country comparison. The lead-lag test asks whether young graduates move before the wider market. Through October 2026 they move with it (graduates and all workers: best match at 0 months, correlation 0.73) **[our calculation]**. No canary has signaled so far; the test cannot see people who are never hired, where Stanford's data point.

Named skills (tagged vocabulary, all 375,045 postings).** A skills list exists on 50.5% of postings that do not mention AI and 81.9% of those that do; postings with a list carry 3.3 skills on average without an AI mention and 4.9 with one. ## 13. AI mentions without AI substance: the AI-washing check (national, postings)
**Definition (ours, a classification, not a finding about any employer).** Of white-collar postings that mention AI, a posting is **boilerplate-only** when we extract no AI skill, no named AI tool and no AI skill group from it: AI appears (for example "we are an AI-powered company" or "uses AI") but the role is not asked to do anything with it that we can detect. It is a risk indicator of AI washing, not proof; our extractor can miss real AI work, and we name no employer. Same frame and date as section 10 (white-collar postings, SOC 11 to 29, live index, Nov 24, 2026). **[our calculation]**; query 4 in `scripts/jobsreport/skills_eq_national.sql`.

| | Postings | Share |
|---|---|---|
| White-collar postings that mention AI | 89,734 | 23.9% of the frame |
| ...with an AI skill, tool or skill group named in the role | 46,667 | 52.0% of AI mentions (12.4% of the frame) |
| ...**boilerplate-only** | 43,067 | **48.0%** of AI mentions (11.5% of the frame) |
| ...name a specific AI tool | 15,523 | 17.3% of AI mentions |

Robustness: counting only postings where we read the full text (74,943 AI-mentioning postings), 46.6% are boilerplate-only; the rest were judged from an excerpt or a duplicate posting and are weaker.

| Boilerplate-only share of AI mentions | Share | AI-mentioning postings |
|---|---|---|
| Entry and early-career | 54.3% | 16,209 |
| Mid-level and senior individual contributors | 43.5% | 49,810 |
| Manager and above | 53.1% | 23,715 |
| Industrial employers | 61.6% | 6,122 |
| Aerospace and defense | 61.3% | 4,004 |
| Life sciences | 52.6% | 2,015 |
| Professional services | 49.6% | 3,587 |
| Healthcare | 48.2% | 5,643 |
| Technology | 45.3% | 44,123 |
| Consumer | 44.1% | 2,746 |
| Financial services | 40.3% | 8,369 |
| Media | 40.2% | 2,525 |

What it shows: about half of the white-collar postings that mention AI ask nothing specific about it, and that share is higher for entry-level and manager-and-above roles and for industrial and defense employers than for technology and finance, where the mentions more often come with a named tool or skill. Technology employers account for 49% of all AI mentions. Industry is the employer's sector, not the job's function. This is one snapshot: we cannot say whether the share is rising or falling, and we do not say AI caused any hiring, pay or layoff outcome.

**Not run:** the other sense of AI washing, employers attributing layoffs to AI. That needs each announcement read (our November layoffs table, section 8, does not record a stated reason), so it is left for the report's layoffs chapter.
