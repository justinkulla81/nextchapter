Since my last note

  • Layoff announcements stayed low: 43,994 in October, the lowest for the month since 2023 and down 71% from October 2025 (Challenger, Gray & Christmas; announcements, not seasonally adjusted).
  • Friday's jobs report was stronger than expected: employers added 165,000 jobs in October and the unemployment rate edged down to 4.1% (BLS, seasonally adjusted).

Today we're publishing the October edition of the NextChapter White Collar Employment Report, a monthly look at how professionals are finding work. This month it adds who is out of work longest, what jobs that ask for AI skills actually pay, and where jobs moved by industry, age and place. The full 22-page report and the data are at launchyournextchapter.com/reports (PDF).

The aim is to track something the standard government releases don't break out: how professionals move from one job to the next. Here is what stood out, starting with the bright spots. Unless noted, changes are month over month and seasonally adjusted; year over year is shown where it adds context.

Bright spots

  • Hiring picked up. Employers added 165,000 jobs in October, against about 65,000 forecasters expected, and the unemployment rate edged down to 4.1% from 4.2%. August and September were revised up by a combined 84,000 jobs; September is now 60,000, up from the 29,000 first reported. Payrolls rose 0.1% on the month and 0.6% over the year (BLS).
  • More openings, fewer layoffs. Job openings rose 4.6% in September to 7.5 million, and the layoff rate fell to 1.0% from 1.1% (JOLTS, seasonally adjusted). The quits rate rose to 2.1% from 1.9%, a sign more workers feel confident enough to move.
  • Professional services and health care are adding the jobs. Professional and business services added 70,000 jobs in October (+0.3%), led by professional, scientific and technical services (+33,000) and administrative and support services (+28,000), and has added 269,000 since a low in October 2025. Over the year health care and social assistance added 519,000 jobs, more than construction, manufacturing, retail and leisure and hospitality combined.
  • Fewer layoffs. Announced layoffs are down about 15% from a year ago, excluding government.
  • Job changers are being rewarded. People who change jobs are getting 5.0% raises, versus 3.6% for those who stay.
  • More openings per job seeker. There is now about one job opening for every unemployed person (1.06), up from 0.94 a year ago.
  • Software job ads are recovering. Indeed's software development postings index is up 23.8% over the year, though still 18% below February 2020. The information sector's job losses are mostly elsewhere: about three-quarters of its 119,000-job decline is telecommunications, data hosting and film, while software publishers are down only about 1% (BLS, Indeed Hiring Lab).
  • Graduates 25 and older are doing well. Unemployment for college graduates aged 25 and older was 2.6% in October, close to September's 2.5% and well below the overall rate (BLS, seasonally adjusted).
  • Business applications are at near-record levels. Applications for new tax IDs were 534,947 in September, up 4.4% over the year and 76% above February 2020 (Census). The catch is below, under clouds.

Clouds

  • Searches are taking longer. 28.5% of unemployed white-collar workers have been out of work six months or more (January to September), up from 23.2% a year earlier, even though white-collar unemployment itself is low (2.63%): the change is in how long people stay out, not how many are out (Census CPS microdata, our calculation, not seasonally adjusted).
  • Experienced professionals face the longest searches. Among unemployed white-collar workers aged 55 to 64, 39.1% have been out six months or more, up from 27.0%, about 4 in 10. It is not mainly retirement: most of them lost a job (68.7%), and the share in long spells rose among job losers, leavers and people re-entering work. Long spells also rose for blue-collar and all workers; the white-collar level is higher (28.5% against 22.7% for blue-collar) but we do not show that the rise is larger.
  • New graduates and older degree-holders face different problems. Recent graduates (22 to 27) have unemployment of 5.44% against 4.32% for all workers, but the shortest searches; degree-holders aged 55 to 64 have low unemployment and the longest searches (37.0% out six months or more against 25.5% for recent graduates). Computer science enrollment fell 12.4% at four-year colleges this fall (National Student Clearinghouse, as reported).
  • Pay is growing more slowly than prices. Annual wage growth slowed to 3.1% in October, from 3.2%, with average hourly earnings up 0.4% on the month. Consumer prices rose 0.6% in September and 3.7% over the year (our calculation from BLS seasonally adjusted CPI).
  • Interest rates may rise. Before Friday's report, Employ America's base case was a Federal Reserve rate increase in December and another in March. A stronger jobs report can make that more likely, and higher borrowing costs tend to slow hiring over time. That is one forecaster's view, not a certainty.
  • Information, finance and federal jobs are shrinking. Over the past year information employment is down 4.2% (119,000 jobs), financial activities down 1.1% (99,000) and federal government down 2.6% (71,000), while total payrolls grew 0.6%. Technology leads announced layoffs this year with 171,501 through October, up 21% from a year earlier (Challenger). Employment in computer and mathematical occupations is estimated to be higher, but long spells among its unemployed workers rose from 27.2% to 41.9%.
  • Hiring is cautious. The hires rate rose only to 3.4% in September, and about 72% of the hiring employers have announced this year is seasonal, concentrated in five companies (Challenger).
  • Large voluntary exit offers. Microsoft offered voluntary exits to 60,000 employees in October. Challenger notes such offers tend to signal more layoffs ahead but give employees a choice and a cushion.
  • More filings to start businesses, fewer that plan to hire. Business applications with planned wages are down 2.8% over the year and 16% over twelve months, so the growth in filings is mostly applications that do not indicate a first payroll; Census projects only about 5% of September's applications will become employer businesses within a year. We do not know why, and household survey data show no move of unemployed professionals into self-employment (3.7% against 3.9% a year earlier).
  • AI's role is hard to pin down. Companies cite AI in about one in five layoff announcements, but in fewer than one in ten of their formal SEC filings. Challenger counts AI as the stated reason for about 20% of announced cuts this year but only 1,265 in October, the lowest month of 2026. Jobs that name AI skills post a median 22% more than similar jobs across employers, but only about 1% more at the same employer, so the observed premium is mainly an employer difference (our own snapshot of open jobs, so a lead, not a verdict). Occupations with higher and lower AI exposure had similar employment changes over the past year (not tested), and we do not claim AI caused any of this.
  • Is AI retraining happening? Offers are plentiful (Labor Department AI literacy framework and a free text-message course, OpenAI and Anthropic academies, university alumni offers), but uptake is poorly measured: the New York Fed found 39% of employed respondents using AI at work and only 15.9% saying their employer offers any AI training.
