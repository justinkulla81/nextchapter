Since my last note

  • Layoff announcements stayed low: 43,994 in October, the lowest for the month since 2023 and down 71% from October 2025 (Challenger, Gray & Christmas; announcements, not seasonally adjusted).
  • Friday's jobs report was stronger than expected: employers added 165,000 jobs in October and the unemployment rate fell to 4.1% (BLS, as reported by CNN).

Today we're launching the NextChapter White Collar Employment Report, a monthly look at how professionals are finding work. The first edition, covering September, is attached.

The aim is to track something the standard government releases don't break out: how professionals move from one job to the next. Here is what stood out, starting with the bright spots. Unless noted, changes are month over month and seasonally adjusted; year over year is shown where it adds context.

Bright spots

  • Hiring picked up. Employers added 165,000 jobs in October, against about 65,000 forecasters expected, and the unemployment rate fell to 4.1% from 4.2%. August and September were revised up by a combined 84,000 jobs; September is now 60,000, up from the 29,000 first reported. Payrolls rose 0.1% on the month and 0.6% over the year (BLS).
  • More openings, fewer layoffs. Job openings rose 4.6% in September to 7.5 million, and the layoff rate fell to 1.0% from 1.1% (JOLTS, seasonally adjusted). The quits rate rose to 2.1% from 1.9%, a sign more workers feel confident enough to move.
  • Professional services are hiring. Professional and business services added 70,000 jobs in October (+0.3%), led by professional, scientific and technical services (+33,000) and administrative and support services (+28,000), and are up 1.2% over the year.
  • Fewer layoffs. Announced layoffs are down about 15% from a year ago, excluding government.
  • Job changers are being rewarded. People who change jobs are getting 5.0% raises, versus 3.6% for those who stay.
  • More openings per job seeker. There is now about one job opening for every unemployed person, up from 0.94 a year ago.
  • Graduates are doing well. Unemployment for college graduates aged 25 and older was 2.6% in October, close to September's 2.5% and well below the overall rate (BLS, seasonally adjusted).
  • New businesses are forming. Business applications are up 11% from a year ago, and AI is making it cheaper to start a company.

Clouds

  • Searches are taking longer. Professionals who lose a job are staying out of work about a third longer than a year ago, even though fewer are losing jobs.
  • Experienced professionals face the longest searches. Among unemployed professionals aged 55 to 64, 39% have been looking for six months or more, up from 27%.
  • Pay is growing more slowly than prices. Annual wage growth slowed to 3.1% in October, from 3.2%, with average hourly earnings up 0.4% on the month. Consumer prices rose 0.6% in September and 3.7% over the year (our calculation from BLS seasonally adjusted CPI).
  • Interest rates may rise. Before Friday's report, Employ America's base case was a Federal Reserve rate increase in December and another in March. A stronger jobs report can make that more likely, and higher borrowing costs tend to slow hiring over time. That is one forecaster's view, not a certainty.
  • Information and finance jobs are shrinking. Over the past year information employment is down 4.2% (119,000 jobs) and financial activities down 1.1% (99,000 jobs), while total payrolls grew 0.6%. Technology leads announced layoffs this year with 171,501 through October, up 21% from a year earlier (Challenger).
  • Hiring is cautious. The hires rate rose only to 3.4% in September, and about 72% of the hiring employers have announced this year is seasonal, concentrated in five companies (Challenger).
  • Large voluntary exit offers. Microsoft offered voluntary exits to 60,000 employees in October. Challenger notes such offers tend to signal more layoffs ahead but give employees a choice and a cushion.
  • Big companies are growing without adding staff. Across 27 of the largest white-collar employers, revenue is up 40% since 2022 while headcount is down slightly. The data can't say whether this reflects productivity, caution or something else.
  • A degree is less of a cushion than it used to be. Long searches have risen faster for college graduates than for other workers, and fastest for people with master's degrees.
  • AI's role is hard to pin down. Companies cite AI in about one in five layoff announcements, but in fewer than one in ten of their formal SEC filings, so its effect on hiring is still difficult to measure. Challenger counts AI as the stated reason for about 20% of announced cuts this year but only 1,265 in October, the lowest month of 2026, as employers increasingly cited economic conditions.
