## 1. Local data centers and the retraining built around them

Data centers are where AI spending meets a town's labor market, and the local picture is about construction and trades more than office work.

- **Jobs.** In the Kansas City area, Meta's Northland site (about $1 billion) is reported at about 1,500 construction workers at peak and about 100 permanent jobs; Nebius's Independence site at about 1,200 construction and about 130 permanent, roughly ten construction jobs for each permanent one **[reported, KCUR/The Beacon, Mar 22, 2026]**. A Brookings county-level study finds roughly 100 to 200 jobs in a typical affected county, data-processing employment up 56% over a decade, wages unchanged and home prices up 2% to 5% **[reported, Brookings, May 4, 2026]**. No source we read gives a national white-collar versus blue-collar split of data-center jobs; the Kansas City article describes construction trades and permanent technicians (starting pay about $67,000 at Google) as blue-collar or technical, and electrical engineers and operations managers as white-collar.
- **Retraining built around data centers.** Louisiana Delta Community College (Monroe) runs two four-week credentials for a nearby Meta site; Meta promised $5 million in scholarships; more than 100 graduates since January, all reported working; student pay about $16.25 an hour, fiber work from about $20; the same article says the construction jobs are expected to largely end in about a decade **[reported, NPR via Connecticut Public, Oct 3, 2026]**. Gateway Technical College (Racine, WI) offers a Microsoft-scholarship data center certificate: 206 enrolled and 14 certificates earned by the end of May **[reported, Route Fifty, Nov 2026]**. Other company-college partnerships (Columbus State with AWS, Maricopa with Microsoft, Northern Virginia CC, Blue Mountain CC) are named without figures. A U.S. Department of Labor grant gave Virginia's community colleges $5 million (a 2023 award) for data center construction and operations pathways **[reported]**. IBEW Local 124 in Kansas City expects its first-year apprentice class to grow from 90 (2021) to about 220 (2026) **[reported]**. We found no state-funded data-center program with a dollar figure.
- **Local fights.** Counties have paused new data centers: Hill County, TX (3-2 vote on May 12, 2026, one year, reportedly the first in Texas) **[reported, Texas Tribune]**; Otoe County, NE (up to one year, May 2026) **[reported, Nebraska Public Media]**; Montgomery County, MD (18 months, July 2026, one advocacy-group source) **[weak]**. The reasons given are water, electricity costs and tax breaks. An Economist/YouGov poll (Aug 28 to 31, 2026) found 63% oppose a new data center in their community and 19% support, and 60% expect higher electricity costs **[reported]**. Virginia's legislative review found its data-center sales-tax exemption costs about $1.9 billion a year and, per a newspaper summary, produces about 84 jobs per $1 million of exemption **[reported; the study itself not read]**.
- **What this does and does not show.** Retraining reaches technicians and tradespeople, and the permanent jobs are few. It does not show any effect on white-collar employment: engineers, architects and project managers do design and management work ahead of construction, and we have no count. We will track open postings for those roles in later editions.

## 2. What the AI companies say about jobs, and what they are paying for

The statements disagree, sometimes inside one company, and the money is pledges and targets, not measured results.

**What they are funding.** OpenAI: certify 10 million Americans by 2030; Academy says more than 4 million "engaged" **[official]**; the jobs platform had not launched as of the page date (Dec 2025). Microsoft Elevate: $4 billion over five years, goal 20 million AI credentials; in Indonesia, more than 2.4 million trained and 1.5 million credentials in two years **[reported/official]**. Amazon Future Ready 2030: $2.5 billion, target 50 million people **[reported]**. Google: says AI will "change far more jobs than it replaces"; 4.1 million people "trained by Google.org-supported organizations" **[official, Google's own page]**. Anthropic: $200 million Economic Futures research fund, mostly grants of $5 million to $30 million; its own post says "the evidence on retraining and job placement is mixed" **[official, July 22, 2026]**. **Every figure is a training, credential or engagement count or a target. We found no placement, employment or wage outcome from any company.**

## 3. The public debate, and how it compares with the data

- **Optimists and warners.** The optimist case (Huang, Altman) is that AI changes tasks and creates new jobs; the warning case (Amodei) is that lasting job loss is possible and policy should prepare, including wage insurance and a safety net **[reported, Fortune]**. Middle-ground economists describe "some job loss, some job change, and some new jobs"; one proposes a rebuilt unemployment-insurance system, retraining in shortage fields, relocation help and no universal basic income **[reported, Platformer, June 2, 2026]**. We could not verify a specific optimist-versus-warner podcast episode from August to November 2026, so we describe the positions by named speaker and outlet, not by episode.
- **What the public thinks.** Majorities expect AI to reduce jobs: 71% in Pew (June 2026; 64% in 2024), 70% in Quinnipiac (March 2026), 79% in Bentley-Gallup (May 2026), highest among young adults **[official]**. People worry less about their own job: 30% of employed adults in Quinnipiac; about 10% in a Boston Fed analysis of the New York Fed survey, against 60% expecting industry-level cuts **[official]**. Quinnipiac found white-collar and blue-collar workers about equally pessimistic (71% and 73%).
- **What independent data show.** Stanford's Digital Economy Lab (Aug 12, 2026): employment for ages 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations is about 19% below where it would be had it tracked less-exposed peers, through fewer hires, and "we do not see widespread, economy-wide job displacement"; the authors call the patterns descriptive, not causal **[official]**. Yale's Budget Lab and Brookings describe a labor market of "stability, rather than disruption" **[official, Oct 2025]**; the New York Fed finds the effect "has more to do with changing skill requirements than eliminating jobs, at least so far" **[official]**; Challenger counts AI as the stated reason for 121,401 of 617,189 announced cuts in 2026 but only 1,265 in October, which is what employers say, not a cause **[reported]**.
- **Our reading, not a finding.** Public worry runs ahead of the aggregate data and matches it in one narrow place, entry-level hiring in exposed jobs. Our own measures (below) show longer searches among unemployed white-collar workers while the white-collar unemployment rate stays under 3%, which fits neither a collapse nor a clean bill of health.

## 4. The canary data we track

We track four monthly Census CPS series back to 2010: new graduates (22 to 27 with a bachelor's degree or more), white-collar workers under 35, white-collar workers 50 and over, and all workers, plus job cuts citing AI by country and a ten-country peer comparison (the early-warning section). The lead-lag test asks whether young graduates and early-career workers move before the wider market. Through October 2026 they move with it, not ahead of it (graduates and all workers: best match at 0 months, correlation 0.73) **[our calculation]**. That finding says no canary has signaled so far. It cannot see people who are never hired, which is where Stanford's data point.

## 5. Cities trying to attract professional and remote workers

- **Tulsa Remote** (OK) pays $10,000 to remote workers who move and stay a year, funded privately. The program reports 3,972 members at the end of 2025, 80% two-year retention and $878 million in direct employment income **[reported, program's own report, May 2026]**. The one outside evaluation, by the Upjohn Institute, is model-based: 58% to 70% of movers would not have come without the incentive and benefits to current residents are more than four times costs, with housing-price offsets noted **[reported]**.
- **Ascend West Virginia** pays $12,000 over two years: 700-plus participants, about 88,000 applications, 96% still in the state after two years (officials), average participant income about $100,000 **[reported, public radio, July 24, 2026]**.
- **Others.** Choose Cumberland (MD) offers $20,000 for up to 10 people **[reported]**; Columbus (GA), Rochester (NY) and Topeka (KS) run similar programs for which we found no independent outcome data **[weak]**. Texas reports 314 corporate headquarters relocating there from 2015 to 2024, announcing 7,360 jobs **[official, Comptroller]**.
- **Training instead of attraction.** New York's AI Jobs Institute (Oct 22, 2026): $10 million of public money, goal to train 40,000 New Yorkers by 2032 **[official]**. We found no U.S. city or state "AI hub" program with a published worker-attraction target and outcomes. Evidence on whether cash-for-movers programs work is thin: apart from Tulsa, program officials supply the figures.

## 6. Money and people: blue-collar and white-collar

- **People (Census CPS, January to September 2026 against 2025, not seasonally adjusted) [our calculation].** White-collar workers (Census occupation groups 1 and 2) are 71.4 million employed, about 44% of all employed people (71.2 million a year earlier); blue-collar workers 32.9 million, about 20% (33.3 million). Unemployment: white-collar 2.63% (2.48%), blue-collar 4.95% (5.04%), all workers 4.32%. Share of the unemployed out 27 weeks or more: white-collar 28.5% (23.2%), blue-collar 22.7% (19.8%). Long-term unemployed: about 551,000 white-collar (420,000) and 389,000 blue-collar (349,000). Blue-collar unemployment is higher; more white-collar people are in long spells. We have not tested the difference between the two groups' rises.
- **Trend lines (BLS projections, 2025 to 2035) [official].** Management +6.2% and median pay $126,520; business and financial +5.5%; computer and mathematical +7.3%; construction and extraction +5.1% ($59,540); production -0.4%; office and administrative support -4.0%; all occupations +3.5% ($50,980).
- **Public dollars [official].** The federal workforce system is occupation-neutral: for program year 2026 the Federal Register lists Adult $875.6 million, Youth $948.1 million and Dislocated Worker $1,396.4 million (a National Skills Coalition table lists $1,095.6 million for Dislocated Worker; we could not reconcile the two), plus about $285 million for apprenticeship grants **[official/reported]**. In program year 2024 the Dislocated Worker program served 187,108 people and 37,119 received training; 69.0% were employed two quarters after exit **[official]**. No official split of public money between blue-collar and white-collar workers exists.
- **Private pledges [reported].** For skilled trades tied to data-center construction: Google.org $50 million to train more than 300,000 workers (June 11, 2026) and Meta $115 million for a workforce academy (June 2026). For professionals we found no pledge of similar size aimed at retraining unemployed professionals; the nearest are general AI-skills pledges (Microsoft, OpenAI, Amazon above) and New York's $10 million institute. We did not search exhaustively.
- **What we could not find.** Dollars or outcomes per worker for either group, and any source on taxes paid or local spending by blue-collar versus white-collar workers. We do not state a contribution comparison.
- **Our reading, not a finding.** Announced investment is concentrated in trades and construction tied to the AI buildout, while the Census data show white-collar workers are a larger share of employment and a larger count of the long-term unemployed. Whether that gap matters for unemployed professionals is unmeasured.
