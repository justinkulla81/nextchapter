## Post 2: Immigration (your profile)

> "Immigrants are taking the professional jobs." We checked.
>
> Using the government's own survey data, we split white-collar long-term unemployment by citizenship:
>
> 🇺🇸 U.S.-born professionals out 6+ months: 0.52% → 0.73% of the workforce (a real rise, beyond the margin of error)
> 🌍 Naturalized citizens: 0.84% → 0.83% (flat)
> 🛂 Noncitizens: their share of white-collar jobs stayed at 6.3%
>
> Meanwhile:
> • H-1B lottery registrations fell 38.5% this year.
> • New international students fell 17%.
> • Only about 85 employers paid the new $100,000 H-1B fee in its first five months.
>
> So immigration didn't cause this year's rise in long searches, and the drop in immigration hasn't fixed it either.
>
> What it does change: with almost no labor-force growth, the economy needs far fewer new jobs to keep unemployment steady (Fed estimates for 2026: under 10,000 to 87,000 a month). A "weak" jobs report means less than it used to.
>
> Full table and sources in the comments.

## Post 3: Productivity vs. long searches (your profile)

> If AI were making companies more productive by pushing professionals out, you'd expect the most productive industries to have the most people stuck job hunting.
>
> We checked. They don't.
>
> 📈 Fastest productivity growth since 2019: management of companies (+3.0% a year), professional and technical services (+2.5%), information (+1.8%).
> 🔍 Long job searches rose in those industries, but none by more than the margin of error.
> 🏥 The only industry with a clear rise in long searches, education and health, had productivity growth near zero.
>
> Meanwhile, across 27 of the largest white-collar employers, revenue grew 40% while headcount fell 1%. That's a selected sample, not the whole economy, but it's the "growth without hiring" pattern people are describing.
>
> So far, productivity gains and long searches aren't moving together industry by industry. Industry averages are blunt tools, though, and we'll repeat this every year.
>
> Where do you see productivity gains actually showing up in your industry: more output, fewer hires, or neither?

## Post 9: The rewiring constraint (your profile; text only, no image)

> AI can now do many professional tasks. So why are AI layoffs still rare?
>
> Our answer from this month's report: three clocks are running at different speeds.
>
> ⚡ Capability is fast. The length of task frontier models can finish has doubled every 4–7 months (METR). On OpenAI's test of expert-level professional tasks, models went from matching experts on under half to about 85% in seven months.
>
> 🏗️ Organizations are slow. About 1 in 5 U.S. firms uses AI at all, and about 95% of those report no headcount change because of it (Census). Workers say AI saves about 2% of all work hours (St. Louis Fed).
>
> 🚪 The labor market adjusts at the hiring gate. Fewer young people hired into exposed jobs. Longer searches for professionals who lose jobs. Not mass layoffs.
>
> The constraint isn't whether AI can do the task. It's whether a company can redesign the whole workflow around it: exceptions, accountability, customers who still want a human, legacy systems.
>
> What it means for reading the news: low AI-layoff counts don't mean "no effect," and benchmark leaps don't mean job losses next quarter. Watch hiring rates and search times.
>
> It's a framework, not proof, and we publish the scorecard of what supports it and what doesn't.
