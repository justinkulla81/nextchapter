## Post 2: The net-productivity insight (2–3 days later; best as a personal post from Justin)

> The economy is getting more productive. The people paying for it aren't the ones collecting.
>
> A rough ledger from this month's NextChapter Displacement Report:
>
> ➕ Output per hour rose 2.2% over the past year. That's roughly $550 billion of extra output.
> ➖ About 135,000 more white-collar workers have been out of work 6+ months than a year ago. That's roughly $25–50 billion of output not being produced.
>
> So the economy comes out 10–20x ahead. But look at who gets what:
> • The gain is spread across 160 million workers and their employers — and labor's share of output is at a record low.
> • The loss lands on a few hundred thousand people, each of whom can lose 1.4–2.8 years of earnings over the following two decades.
>
> Net positive for the economy. Sharply negative for people in transition.
>
> It's an estimate, not a measurement, and we can't attribute either side to AI. The assumptions are in the report.
>
> Who should carry the cost of the transition — the people in it, their former employers, or all of us?

## Post 3: The hiring-yield chart (2–3 days after Post 2; attach a screenshot of the report's hires-per-opening chart)

> In 2005, employers made about 1.3 hires for every job they listed.
> Today it's 0.73.
>
> Fewer hires per opening. More than twice as many applications per opening as in 2022. Longer searches for the people applying.
>
> That's the hiring market professionals are facing — and why "just apply to more jobs" stopped working.
>
> (Caveat we put in the report: hires and openings aren't matched one-to-one, and some of the decline reflects cheaper online postings. The trend is still clear.)
