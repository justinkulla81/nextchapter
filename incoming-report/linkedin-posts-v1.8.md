## Post 2: Jobs are growing, openings match job seekers (your profile)

> Two numbers that don't usually appear in the same headline:
>
> • The economy added 612,000 jobs in the first nine months of 2026, against 232,000 in the same months of 2025.
> • September's gain was only 29,000, and July was revised to a loss.
>
> Both are true. Slow monthly gains matter less than they used to because the labor force is growing slowly: Federal Reserve estimates of the job growth needed to hold unemployment steady are now between under 10,000 and 87,000 a month.
>
> And openings are close to balanced: 1.01 openings per unemployed person in August, up from 0.94 a year ago. New jobless claims are 197,000 a week, against 225,000 a year ago.
>
> What is slow: hiring. That is where the next post goes.

## Post 4: Starting businesses, and employed professionals are holding steady (your profile)

> We checked two things about people who already have jobs or are starting something.
>
> 🏢 Business applications were 531,728 in August, up 11.4% from a year ago. Applications likely to hire employees were up 31% since 2019.
> 🧑‍💼 Among employed managers and professionals, the share working part time but wanting full time (1.37%), holding a second job (6.19%) and self-employed (11.29%) were flat or lower than in 2025.
>
> So the adjustment for professionals who keep their jobs is not showing up inside those jobs. It shows up in how long a search takes for those who leave one.
>
> Limit worth knowing: the monthly survey cannot see someone moved from employee to contractor at the same company, and filing for a business is not the same as earning a living from one. Both are in the report.

## Post 5: What public programs are adding: reemployment services (company page)

> One public reemployment program has been tested with a randomized trial.
>
> Rhode Island's RESEA program (federally funded, run by the state labor department) requires selected unemployment-insurance claimants to meet with staff for an eligibility check, labor market information and a reemployment plan.
>
> In a 23,549-claimant trial run by researchers at Harvey Mudd, the University of Maryland and UNC:
>
> ⏱️ 1.99 fewer weeks on benefits (95% interval 1.75 to 2.24)
> 💼 1.47 percentage points higher reemployment
> 💵 $1,153 higher annualized wages (3.5%)
> 🏦 About $7.9 million less in benefits paid, against a $2.99 million program cost (authors' estimate)
>
> Limits: one state, all claimants rather than professionals only.
>
> This is one of eight programs in a new chapter of our report, each listed with who runs it, its scale and its evidence. For the newest programs, the evidence is "none yet."

## Post 7: Where the transition is slower (your profile)

> The same report that shows jobs growing also shows where the transition is slower, with the size of each.
>
> 🔎 White-collar workers out of work 27+ weeks: 0.75% of the labor force, up from 0.56% a year ago (beyond the margin of error).
> 👴 Among unemployed white-collar workers aged 55–64, 39% have been looking 27+ weeks, up from 27%.
> 💻 Computer and math occupations: 41% of the unemployed have been looking 27+ weeks, up from 28%.
> 💲 Of long-tenured workers displaced in 2023–2025, 66% were reemployed by January 2026 and about half of those earn less than before.
>
> These are measures of how long a search takes, not why. We do not claim AI caused them, and the report lists the other explanations we considered.
>
> If you are in one of these groups, the practical lesson is to start earlier and use every support available. Which support helped you most?

## Post 8: States publishing data (company page)

## Post 10: Productivity (your profile)

> Output per hour rose 2.5% in 2025. What sits behind that number?
>
> We broke down the San Francisco Fed's data:
>
> ⚙️ "Underlying" productivity (total factor productivity, adjusted for how hard existing workers and machines are run): +0.3% in 2025, −0.3% over the past year
> 📈 Most of the output-per-hour gain came from output rising while hours worked barely grew (+0.2%)
>
> So the economy is producing more with barely more hours. That is growth without much hiring, and not yet visible as faster underlying technological progress. Electricity and computers each took a decade or more to show up in aggregate productivity.
>
> We will track this each quarter.
